**The five agents**
1. **Capture every inbound lead.** Collects leads from forms, site chat, shared inboxes, and ad lead forms, removes duplicates, and enriches records. Output: one clean record with source attribution. Hands off when the lead belongs to an open opportunity, going to that owner.
2. **Qualify against your own rules.** Scores fit and intent using your criteria, asks follow-up questions, and explains each score. Inputs: ICP definition, territory rules, past win data. Hands off when the score crosses your threshold or the lead asks for a person.
3. **Follow up in your voice.** Runs sequences over email, SMS, WhatsApp, and optionally voice, using an approved message library, and stops when the prospect replies. Guardrails: approved messages, send windows, opt-out handling. Hands off when a reply raises pricing, contract terms, or anything outside approved answers.
4. **Book the right rep.** Finds times that suit the prospect and rep, sends invites, handles reschedules, and writes a call brief. Hands off when the account needs a named owner or executive sponsor.
5. **Keep the CRM true.** Writes activities, stages, scores, notes, and owner changes to the CRM in real time. Output: an audit trail. Hands off when a field conflicts with a manual edit, flagging the conflict without overwriting the rep's change.

**How are AI agents kept within bounds?** Agents work within limits set before launch: an approved message library, topics that always go to a person, optional approval before sending, and send limits. Every action is logged. Hosting regions, model data use, subprocessors, and certifications are in a security pack shared under NDA.

**Tenant isolation:** Each customer gets a logically separated workspace. Business units, regions, or brands can have separate workspaces with their own data, playbooks, and permissions.

**Access and audit:** Role-based access separates admins, managers, and reps. Every agent action is written to an audit log and to the affected CRM record. Admins control who can edit playbooks and hand-off rules. Managers approve new message templates before they are sent. Reps see the full history of every lead handed to them.

**How agents are kept in bounds:**

**Speed to lead: why the first reply decides the deal** (applyd.ai, 5 November 2026, 4-minute read). Condensed overview from the fetch tool, not verbatim.

Core claim: "A new lead is at its warmest in the minutes after they ask."

**1. What the first reply does**
- It arrives while the lead still remembers their question and why they asked.
- It sets the frame that later suppliers get compared against.
- It signals what working with the company is like. A prompt, specific reply suggests an organised team, and silence suggests the opposite.

**2. Why leads go cold**
Leads usually cool because attention moves on and context fades, not because the buyer changed their mind. Delays often come from ordinary causes: shared inboxes, reps in meetings, or leads arriving outside working hours.

**3. What "fast" should mean**
A generic auto-reply doesn't count, since buyers read it as a brush-off. A good first reply should:
1. Acknowledge the specific thing the lead asked about.
2. Ask one or two questions that decide the next step.
3. Offer a clear next step, such as a time to talk.
4. Make it easy to reach a person.

**4. Where agents help and where they should stop**
AI agents can reply to every lead immediately, ask the team's qualifying questions, and record the answers. They should stop at matters needing judgment: pricing, discounts, contract terms, and anything marked sensitive. The goal is to route the right lead to the right rep with context gathered, not to have an agent make commitments.

**5. How to measure it**
Measure before changing anything:
- Time from form submission to first human or agent reply, by lead source.
- The same figure split by working hours, evenings, and weekends.
- The share of leads that never received a reply.
- Meetings held as a share of leads, so speed is judged by outcomes.

Start with the slowest sources and time windows.

**6. A fair way to test it**
Run agents on a share of real leads, keep the rest with the team, and compare time to first reply, meetings booked, and how often reps had to correct something.

**How to write hand-off rules for AI agents** — 5 November 2026 · 4 min read · by the applyd.ai team. Condensed by the fetch tool, not verbatim.

Lead: "An agent is only as safe as the rules that say when it stops."

**Introduction:** Teams using AI agents on inbound leads must decide when a person takes over. If that answer exists only in people's heads, the agent will find the gaps. Written, reviewed, changeable rules make the system trustworthy. The guide offers a structure for writing them and lists common failure points.

**What a hand-off rule is:** A sentence pairing a trigger with a consequence: when X happens, a person takes over. It should use the sales team's everyday language and be owned by the people accountable to the customer, not by whoever configures the software.

**The five parts of a rule:** A rule is complete only when it answers all five questions:
1. **Trigger:** what the agent detects, such as a topic, a score above a threshold, a request for a person, or an account type.
2. **Owner:** who takes over, by role or name, and what happens if they are unavailable.
3. **What is passed on:** a conversation summary, the lead's answers so far, and any commitments the agent has made.
4. **What the agent says meanwhile:** a brief, honest message that a person will follow up, without guessing at an answer.
5. **Limit:** how long before the lead is reminded or someone else is alerted, so a hand-off never dead-ends.

**Template (one block per rule):**
- **When:** the lead asks about pricing, discounts or contract terms.
- **Hand to:** the account owner; if unavailable, the team lead.
- **Pass on:** a summary, the lead's answers, and the agent's questions.
- **Agent says:** that a colleague will pick this up and when to expect a reply.
- **Escalate if:** no reply within the time the team sets.

**Rules most teams need first:**
- Pricing, discounts and contract questions go to a person.
- High-fit accounts above a team-chosen score get a rep for the first call.
- Topics marked sensitive by compliance or legal go to a person.
- A lead who asks for a person gets one.
- A manager approves new message templates before the agent sends them.
- An agent never overwrites a rep's manual CRM edit.

Routine work can stay with the agent: answering common questions from approved replies, collecting basic details, and booking calendar time. This split frees reps for conversations that need them.

**Mistakes that make rules fail:**
- Goal-based rules (such as "hand off when it seems important") instead of testable triggers.
- Rules with no owner, sending leads to a queue nobody monitors.
- Rules written once and never reviewed as products, pricing and teams change.
- Too many rules at launch. Start with the few that protect you and add more as data shows where agents are comfortable.

**Testing before go-live:**
- Replay a sample of past leads, including difficult ones, and check what the agent would have done under each rule. Where the team would have acted differently, adjust the rule or wording and rerun the sample.
- Start with a share of live leads and keep a way to switch agents off.
- Review the hand-offs: were they to the right person, with enough context, and fast enough? A rule that fires too often wastes rep time; one that fires too rarely is a risk.

**Keeping rules readable and changeable:** A new manager should be able to read the rule set in ten minutes and say what the agents will and won't do. Keep the list short, name an owner, review it on a schedule, and keep the rules under your control.

**Evaluating AI SDR vendors: questions your security team will ask** (applyd.ai, 5 November 2026, 4-minute read). Condensed by the fetch tool, not verbatim.

This guide argues that buying an AI sales agent is both a software and a security decision, since the agent reads lead data, writes to your CRM, and sends messages in your name. It recommends involving a sales leader, a security reviewer, and a rep early, and asking vendors for written answers.

**Questions to ask, by group:**

1. **Data and access:** Which systems the agent connects to and with what permissions, whether access can start read-only, how customer data is separated, who at the vendor can see lead data and whether that access is logged, and how data is deleted when you leave.
2. **Hosting, models and subprocessors:** Where data is stored and processed by region, which AI models are used and whether your data trains them, which subprocessors handle data and how you learn of changes, and which certifications the vendor holds (ask for evidence).
3. **What the agent can and cannot do:** Whether you can write and change hand-off rules yourself, whether the agent sends only approved messages, which actions need approval and what send limits apply, whether it can negotiate, discount or commit on your behalf, and whether it can overwrite manual CRM edits.
4. **CRM and reps:** Whether sync runs both ways and which side wins conflicts, what happens if the connection drops mid-update, and whether you can see exactly which fields the agent may change.
5. **Audit and traceability:** Whether every action is logged with its reason, whether a manager can view everything sent and written for one lead, and whether logs can be exported.
6. **How the vendor tests its agents:** Whether agents are tested on your past leads and who defines a good outcome, whether regression checks guard against changes to prompts or models, how prompt injection and data leaks are tested (the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications is cited as a reference), and what is monitored after launch (quality, speed, cost per action).
7. **Rollout and exit:** Whether you can pilot on a share of leads against your team, whether you can switch agents off quickly, and how fast the vendor reports security incidents affecting you.

**Using the answers:** Score every vendor against the same list and share it with security early. The strongest signals are specific answers, commitments in writing, and a plain statement of what the vendor cannot do.
